1

The Sun heats unevenly — and the air moves

Stand on a beach on a sunny afternoon and you'll feel it: a cool wind blowing in off the sea, even when the morning was dead calm. Nobody switched it on. The Sun did.

Sunlight warms the land fast. The sea hardly warms at all — water stores enormous amounts of heat and stirs it metres deep. So by mid-morning the air over the land is warmer, lighter, and starts to rise. Cooler, heavier air from over the sea slides in underneath to take its place. Up high, the air flows back out to sea and sinks. A loop.

That loop is the whole secret of wind: uneven heating makes uneven pressure, and air flows from high pressure to low. Every wind on Earth — from a beach breeze to a hurricane to the jet stream — is a version of this.

Let the day run and watch the breeze switch on by itself around mid-morning. Then turn the sun down to thick cloud and start again at dawn. After sunset, watch it turn around.

The sea breeze is a heat engine you can feel on your face. Warm air rising over land is the "low"; cool sea air is the "high"; the wind is air flowing from one to the other.

At night the land cools faster than the sea and the whole thing runs backwards — a gentle land breeze blowing out to sea. Fishing boats used it for millennia: out at dawn on the land breeze, home in the afternoon on the sea breeze.

2

Highs, lows, and the slope in between

You live at the bottom of an ocean of air. About ten tonnes of it press down on every square metre of you — that weight is air pressure, around 1013 hectopascals (hPa) at sea level.

It is never quite even. Where air is sinking or piling up, the pressure is a little higher: a high. Where it is warming and rising, or being drawn away aloft, the pressure is a little lower: a low. The differences are tiny — a deep storm is only 3–5% below normal — but air feels them, and slides from high towards low like water running downhill.

Weather maps draw this slope as isobars: lines joining places with the same pressure, like contour lines on a hiking map. Where they crowd together, the slope is steep and the push is strong.

Pull the high and the low closer together, or deepen the difference, and watch the isobars squeeze and the air speed up. Notice where the air ends up: piling into the low, with nowhere to go but up.

Wind is air flowing down a pressure slope. The closer the isobars, the steeper the slope, the stronger the wind. Forecasters read a weather map exactly like this.

But look at a real map and you'll see something odd: the wind blows almost along the isobars, not across them — and lows last for days instead of filling up in hours. Something is pushing the air sideways…

3

Why storms spin

The ground under you is turning. At 50° latitude it carries you east at about 1,000 km/h, and — less obviously — it slowly rotates around the spot you stand on, like a very slow merry-go-round: once a day at the poles, not at all at the equator.

Roll a ball straight across a spinning merry-go-round and, to anyone riding it, the ball curves away. Nothing pushed it sideways; the floor turned underneath. Air moving across the spinning Earth does the same: in the northern hemisphere it veers right, in the southern hemisphere left. This is the Coriolis effect, and its strength is f = 2Ω sin(latitude).

So air aiming for a low never gets there directly. It is turned aside, again and again, until it is circling the low — anticlockwise in the north, clockwise in the south. That is every cyclone, hurricane and typhoon on Earth.

Roll the ball, then switch to riding the merry-go-round. On the map, slide from the north pole to the south and back — or jump to , , . Then switch friction to "high up" and watch the air stop falling in.

High up, the inward push and the Coriolis turn balance, and air flows along the isobars (the geostrophic wind). That's why lows last for days: the spin keeps the air from filling them.

Near the ground friction spoils the balance, so the air spirals in — and in the middle it has to rise. Rising air makes cloud (chapter 5). That's why a low means rain.

And no — the Coriolis effect doesn't decide which way your sink drains. Over a few centimetres and seconds it's millions of times too weak; the shape of the sink wins.

4

Friction: slow, gusty and twisted near the ground

Climb a tower, a hill or a wind turbine and the wind gets stronger with every metre. The ground drags on the air: grass a little, forests and cities a lot. The lowest air is held back; the air above it is held back by that air, and so on up — a few hundred metres of slowed, tumbling air called the boundary layer.

The drag doesn't just slow the wind, it churns it. Air tripping over trees and rooftops rolls into eddies, and those eddies are what you feel as gusts. Over the smooth sea there's little to trip on, so sea winds are stronger and steadier — which is why the windiest places are coasts, islands and hilltops, and why wind farms go offshore.

Friction also explains chapter 3's spiral: slower air feels a weaker Coriolis turn, so near the ground the wind cuts across the isobars toward the low.

Keep the same wind aloft and switch from sea to city. Watch the wind near the ground fade — and the gusts grow.

The same weather system gives very different winds depending on what's underneath it. Over the sea the wind near the surface keeps about 70% of its speed aloft; over a city barely a quarter — but in sudden, much stronger gusts.

7

The global engine

Zoom out from the beach and the planet runs the same trick on a giant scale. The tropics get far more sunshine than the poles, so the air is always trying to carry heat poleward. If Earth didn't spin, it would do it in one loop: rise at the equator, flow to the pole up high, sink, and creep back along the ground.

But Earth does spin, and the Coriolis effect from chapter 3 breaks that loop into three cells in each hemisphere. Air rising in the tropics sinks again around 30°. As it flows back toward the equator it is deflected west — the steady trade winds. Air heading poleward from 30° is deflected east — the westerlies. Near 60° it meets cold polar air, and that clash is where most of our storms are born.

  • Rising air (the equator, ~60°): cloud and rain.
  • Sinking air (~30°, the poles): clear, dry skies.
  • Surface winds: the return flow, bent by Earth's spin.

High up, where the cells meet, the air races east in narrow rivers: the jet streams. They steer the lows and fronts of chapters 3 and 6 — which is why weather in Europe and North America mostly arrives from the west.

Find your own latitude and read off your prevailing wind. Run the months and watch the rain belt follow the Sun north and south. Then switch off Earth's spin.

Look at a map of the world's great deserts — the Sahara, Arabia, the Kalahari, the Australian outback, the Atacama, the Sonoran. They line up near 30° north and south, right where the Hadley cells' air comes back down, warmed and dried.

Sailors knew the pattern long before anyone explained it: ride the trade winds west across the Atlantic, come home on the westerlies further north, and dread the windless doldrums and "horse latitudes" in between.
