Why a day is 24 hours (and also isn't)

Day and night are simple: the Earth is a ball lit from one side, and it spins. Your side turns into the sunlight (sunrise), across it (noon), and out again (sunset).

But here's a puzzle. Measure one full turn of the Earth against the distant stars and you get 23 hours 56 minutes. Yet noon-to-noon takes 24 hours. Where do the extra four minutes come from?

While the Earth spins, it's also travelling along its orbit. After one full turn, the Sun is no longer quite in the same direction — so the Earth has to turn a little bit more to bring the Sun back overhead.

Watch the red dot (you, at noon). Then shrink the year to a handful of days to make the effect huge, or make the Earth spin the other way, like Venus. Switch to standing on the equator to watch a star beat the Sun back to the same spot in the sky.

In one year the Earth spins about 366¼ times relative to the stars — but we only see 365¼ sunrises. Going once around the Sun "uses up" one whole turn.

Astronomers call the star-to-star turn the sidereal day; our clocks run on the solar day. Both are real. They're just measured against different things — the first hint that motion depends on what you measure it against.

2

Seasons: it's the tilt, not the distance

Ask people why summer is warm and many say: "because we're closer to the Sun." It's a very reasonable guess. It's also not the reason. The Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January — in the middle of the northern winter.

The real reason is that the Earth's spin axis is tilted by 23.4°, and it keeps pointing the same way in space (towards the North Star) all year round.

In June the northern half leans towards the Sun. Sunlight hits it more directly — concentrated on less ground — and the Sun stays up longer. Double win: stronger sunlight, for more hours. Six months later the same tilt leans the north away, and the south gets its summer.

The distance does change — by about 3%, giving roughly 7% more sunlight in January than in July. That's real, but it's small next to the tilt effect, which more than triples a mid-latitude's daily sunlight between winter and summer.

If distance caused the seasons, the whole planet would have summer at the same time. Instead, when it's summer in Amsterdam it's winter in Sydney — opposite seasons in opposite hemispheres is the giveaway that it's the tilt.

The yellow and blue lines on the globe are the tropics and polar circles: they sit exactly at the tilt angle. Change the tilt and they move — they're not geography, they're geometry.

The Moon is always half lit

The Moon makes no light of its own. The Sun always lights exactly half of it — the half facing the Sun. As the Moon circles us every 29½ days, we see that lit half from different angles: all of it (full Moon), none of it (new Moon), and everything in between.

It is not Earth's shadow, as is often thought. Earth's shadow points straight away from the Sun, and the Moon only passes through it during a lunar eclipse.

Which side of the Moon is lit — right, left, top or bottom — depends on where you are and when you look. The rule never changes: the lit side points at the Sun, even when the Sun is below the horizon. In the evening, a growing Moon's lit side points down towards where the Sun has set: lower right seen from Europe, lower left from Australia, and straight down near the equator, where a crescent lies on its back like a smile. As the Moon arcs across the sky during the night, its lit side keeps pointing at the hidden Sun — so it slowly turns.

And it always shows us the same face. That's not because it doesn't spin — it spins exactly once per orbit. Billions of years ago, Earth's gravity raised tides in the young Moon's rock, and the friction braked its spin until it matched its orbit. This is called tidal locking.

The same tides are still at work on us: the Moon's pull on our oceans (see Understanding Waves) slowly brakes Earth's spin — days get about 2 milliseconds longer per century — and pushes the Moon 3.8 cm further away every year.

There is no permanent "dark side of the Moon". There's a far side we never see from Earth — and at new Moon, it's in full sunshine.

The full Moon rises as the Sun sets, because it's opposite the Sun. A thin evening crescent sets soon after the Sun, because it's next to it. Once you see the geometry, you can tell the time from the Moon.

Why isn't there an eclipse every month?

Every month the Moon passes between us and the Sun (new Moon), and two weeks later it's on the far side of us (full Moon). So why don't we get a solar eclipse every new Moon, and a lunar eclipse every full Moon?

Because the Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5° compared with Earth's orbit around the Sun. Most months, the Moon slips just above or just below the Sun, and just above or below Earth's shadow.

The two orbits cross at two points, the nodes. An eclipse needs a new or full Moon to happen while the Sun lines up with the nodes. That alignment comes round about every 173 days — the eclipse seasons. That's why eclipses come in pairs: a solar and a lunar eclipse often happen two weeks apart.

The nodes themselves slowly turn, once every 18.6 years, so eclipse seasons drift through the calendar.

Watch from the Sun's direction: every two weeks the Moon slides past — usually above or below. Let the year run and watch for the flash. Then flatten the Moon's orbit to — eclipses every two weeks — and back to the real .

Five degrees doesn't sound like much. But the Moon is 384,000 km away, so 5° moves it up to 35,000 km above or below the Sun–Earth line — while Earth's shadow there is only about 9,000 km wide. Usually it misses.

There's one more lucky coincidence: the Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon, but also about 400 times farther away. That's why the Moon can just cover the Sun in a total eclipse.

5

An orbit is falling — and missing

Why doesn't the Moon fall down? It does. It's falling towards Earth right now, and so is the International Space Station. They're just moving sideways so fast that they keep missing.

Isaac Newton imagined a cannon on a mountain so tall it pokes above the air. Fire slowly and the ball curves down and lands. Fire faster and it lands farther away. Fire fast enough and the ground curves away beneath it as fast as it falls. It never lands. That's an orbit.

Near Earth's surface, the planet curves down about 5 metres for every 8 km you travel. And in one second, anything falls about 5 metres. So at about 8 km per second sideways, falling and curving cancel out. Low orbits really are that fast: the ISS circles the Earth every 92 minutes.

Astronauts float not because there's no gravity up there — at the ISS it's still about 90% as strong as on the ground — but because they and their station are falling together, all the time.

Kepler's three rules of the road

Around 1609, Johannes Kepler pored over years of careful observations of Mars and found three rules that every orbit follows. Seventy years later, Newton showed they all come from one thing: gravity weakening with distance.

  • Ellipses. Orbits are ovals, with the Sun not in the middle but at one focus, off to the side.
  • Equal areas in equal times. Closer in, a planet speeds up; far out, it slows down. A line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.
  • Bigger orbit, slower year. The square of the period grows with the cube of the distance. Far planets have farther to go and move more slowly.

Earth's orbit is very nearly a circle — just 1.7% off-centre — but even that shows: we move about 3% faster in January than in July. That's why, counting from equinox to equinox, the northern autumn-and-winter half of the year (September to March, 179 days) is about a week shorter than spring-and-summer (186 days).

Nothing pushes a planet along its orbit. It speeds up near the Sun for the same reason a ball speeds up rolling downhill — it's falling inward — and slows down climbing back out. An orbit is a never-ending trade between height and speed.

Why Mars sometimes goes backwards

Night after night, Mars drifts slowly eastward against the stars. But every couple of years it stops, goes backwards for about two months, stops again, and carries on. For more than a thousand years, astronomers built ever more complicated machinery of circles-on-circles to explain these loops.

The real explanation is that we're moving too. Earth is on an inside lane, going faster. Every 26 months we catch up with Mars and overtake it.

It's like passing a slower car on the motorway: for a moment, against the distant hills, it seems to slide backwards — even though both cars are going forward. Nothing about Mars changes. Only our point of view does.

Let it run from above and watch the sight line sweep. Then switch to Mars in Earth's sky to see the loop among the real constellations — and to seen from the Sun, where it vanishes.

Mars goes backwards exactly when Earth overtakes it — which is also when it's closest and brightest in our sky (an opposition). The loop isn't in Mars's motion. It's in ours.

This is one of the oldest lessons in astronomy, and the theme of the next chapter: what you see depends on where you're watching from.

Launch day: why that date?

A rocket can't just leave whenever it's ready. The target is moving, and so are we. After a few minutes of engine burn, a spacecraft coasts almost the whole way — falling around the Sun (or the Earth), exactly like the cannonball in chapter 5. That fall only ends at the target if you leave when everything is lined up. That moment is called a launch window.

Some windows open every 26 months, some once a month, some for one second a day — and one opened once in 175 years.

Each path drawn here is calculated, not sketched by hand: given where Earth is on launch day and where the target will be on arrival day, there is exactly one orbit around the Sun that connects them (this is called Lambert's problem). Real missions add small course corrections along the way; those are left out.

Pick a launch. Each one jumps to its real date — let time run and watch it fly. Drag and zoom as everywhere else.

A launch date isn't picked from a calendar. It's the moment the solar system opens a door — and the size of the door depends on where you're going: weeks every 26 months for Mars, about a day a month for an Apollo landing site, one second for a space station.

How fast are you going? It depends who's asking

Sit perfectly still. You are spinning with the Earth at up to 1,670 km/h. The Earth is carrying you around the Sun at 107,000 km/h. The Sun is carrying all of us around the Milky Way at roughly 830,000 km/h — one lap every 230 million years. And the whole galaxy is moving through the universe.

So what's your real speed? There isn't one. Every one of those numbers is true — each measured against something different. Speed only means something relative to something else.

Why don't we feel any of it? Because you can't feel steady motion — only changes in motion. Our turns are so enormous that the changes are tiny. (It's the same reason you can pour a drink on a smoothly cruising plane.)

The closest thing to a universal yardstick is the cosmic microwave background, the leftover glow of the Big Bang that fills all of space. Against it, the Sun moves at about 370 km/s (1.3 million km/h) towards the constellations Leo and Crater, and the Milky Way as a whole at about 600 km/s — over 2 million km/h.

You may have seen videos of the planets corkscrewing behind the Sun like the tail of a comet. The real picture is stranger and more beautiful. Earth's orbit is tilted about 60° to our path through the galaxy, and the coils are stretched out — each one is about 25 times longer than it is wide.

Tap each motion to switch the frame of reference: Earth, Sun, galaxy, cosmic background. Same Earth, four completely different paths. In the galaxy view, try squeezing the coils.

A circle, an ellipse, a stretched spring: these are all the same motion, described from different places. None of them is "the real one".

And the speeds don't simply add up: they point in different directions. Relative to the cosmic background, your total speed swings between about 340 and 400 km/s over a year, depending on whether Earth's orbit carries you with or against the Sun's motion. Satellites have measured this yearly wobble.
